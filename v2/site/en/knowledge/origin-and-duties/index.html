Origin of goods and duties in EU–Mexico trade

Updated: 4 October 2026

How to prove preferential origin, which documents you may need and why it pays to have them ready before shipment.

The same European product can enter Mexico duty-free or pay tens of percent. Often a single document makes the difference. Since 2000, trade between the EU and Mexico has been governed by a free trade agreement, known in Mexico as TLCUEM. Thanks to it, many EU goods enter Mexico duty-free or at a reduced rate, but only if they come with proof of origin. Without it the general rate applies, which since early 2026 has been high for many goods and can reach tens of percent.

The rules of origin provide for three kinds of proof:

  • an origin declaration on the invoice, for consignments up to EUR 6,000,
  • an EUR.1 movement certificate issued by the customs authority of the exporting country, for any value,
  • an invoice declaration by an approved exporter, for any value.

The EUR 6,000 threshold only determines the type of document. It doesn't create any obligation to pay duty.

The EUR.1 is issued by the exporting country's customs; in Poland, for example, it is free and usually ready within a day or two. The customs broker in Mexico needs the original; a scan won't do. Approved exporter status is more convenient: you obtain it once and then print a fixed statement on every invoice. It isn't a REX number. The REX system doesn't apply to trade with Mexico, and an invoice with a declaration in that format won't secure the preference.

"Made in Germany" or "Made in Poland" on a label doesn't yet mean the goods originate in the EU for the purposes of the agreement. What counts is where the materials come from and whether they underwent sufficient processing in the EU. The agreement has a separate rule for each heading, and some products don't meet it even though they're made in Europe. If the exporter isn't the manufacturer, it needs origin declarations from its suppliers.

Not all EU goods are duty-free today. Some tariff codes pay the full rate or only a reduced one. Online duty calculators don't always reflect this, so we check the rate in the legal text and with the customs broker.

In May 2026 the EU and Mexico signed two new agreements: the Modernised Global Agreement and the Interim Trade Agreement. The second matters more for businesses, because it covers trade and can enter into force earlier, without waiting for ratification by every EU member state. On the EU side, the procedure was completed in July 2026.

Mexico hasn't completed its part yet. According to information from the end of September 2026, the documents had not yet been formally sent to the Mexican Senate. The interim agreement will enter into force on the first day of the second month after both sides notify each other that their procedures are complete, so there is no firm date yet. Until then, the rules described above apply.

Legal basis

  • Decision No 2/2000 of the EU–Mexico Joint Council, Annex III
  • Acuerdo of 31.08.2022 (DOF), duty rates for goods originating in the EU
  • EU–Mexico Interim Trade Agreement, Official Journal of the EU of 31.07.2026
